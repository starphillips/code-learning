
**O(1)**: The time doesn't change with input size.  
Example: `arr[5]`, a hash map lookup, `pop()` on an array, or plain maths like `8 * 2`.

**O(log n)**: Throw away half of what's left each step until you reach 1.  
Example: binary search. A billion items takes only about 30 steps.

**O(√n)**: Only go up to the square root of n. It's rare in interviews.  
Example: checking whether a number is prime, or finding its factors.

**O(n)**: If the input doubles, the time doubles.  
Example: a single loop, or summing an array.

**O(n log n)**: Do a log n job n times.  
Example: sorting. If you sort and then do one loop, it's still O(n log n), because the biggest term wins.

**O(n²)**: A loop inside a loop over the same data.  
Example: comparing every item to every other item. If the two loops are over different arrays, it's O(a · b).

For the range of the array, for each element in the array: we do this

**O(2ⁿ)**: The work doubles with every extra item, usually because each item has 2 choices (in or out).  
Example: listing all subsets. 10 items gives 1,024 subsets and 30 items gives over a billion.

**O(n!)**: Every possible ordering of the items.  
Example: all permutations. 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. If your solution is this, it's too slow for anything but tiny inputs.

**Order from fastest to slowest:** O(1), O(log n), O(√n), O(n), O(n log n), O(n²), O(2ⁿ), O(n!)

**Two quick rules:**

- Drop constants and small terms: `O(2n + 5)` is just `O(n)`.
- One loop after another adds. A loop inside a loop multiplies.



|A line that...|Costs|
|---|---|
|Sets a variable, does maths, reads `arr[i]`, `push`, `pop`, map/set lookup|1|
|Loops through the array|n|
|Loops with the variable doubling or halving|log n|
|Loops with `i * i <= n`|√n|
|Copies, searches or slices an array (`includes`, `indexOf`, `slice`, `[...arr]`)|n|
|Sorts|n log n|
